# ADR 0016: "Run demo": on-demand, stateless steps, and the Neon compute arithmetic

Status: accepted

**Context.** An order only advanced while a GitHub Actions simulator burst ran, up to 30 minutes away: a poor demo. A background process that moves orders would keep Neon compute awake. The Neon free plan allows 100 compute-hours a month per project, and compute stays awake for about 5 minutes after the last query (the README's assumption, not checked in the console).

**Decision.** The visitor's own browser is the clock. Once an order is paid, the order page shows "Run demo". While it runs and the tab is visible, the page calls `POST /api/orders/:id/demo/step` about every 2.5 s, one at a time. It stops when the tab is hidden, the order finishes, an error comes back, or 6 minutes pass. Nothing runs when nobody is looking.

A step (`DemoRunner` in `packages/engine/src/demo.ts`) is stateless and idempotent:
- **Time comes from a demo clock stored on the order**, not from how many calls arrived: each step adds `min(now - last_step_at, 6 s)` to `order_demos.demo_ms` in one guarded `UPDATE`. Two tabs add up to wall time, not double (tested); a hidden tab cannot make the car jump (the 6 s cap).
- **Time compression is 10x** (`DEMO_TIME_COMPRESSION`), shown in the UI as "Demo speed". Stage times in simulated seconds: station accepts after 15, driver sets off 45 s after being assigned, drives the route at its estimated duration, 30 s at the door, 240 s delivering. A 6 km trip with a 10-minute route estimate plays in 97.5 s; 1.5 km in 57.5 s; 11.5 km (18 minutes) in 145 s (measured in PGlite with a fake router; model-driven, not a claim about OSRM's real durations).
- Each step applies whatever transitions are due through `engine.submit` (station accept, normal dispatch, depart, arrive, start, complete) and sends one location ping through `engine.recordLocation`, so the Durable Object broadcasts it to every viewer. Transitions are guarded by the state machine and the version check, so double clicks and racing tabs cannot apply one twice.
- **Demo drivers** (`demo-drv-01` to `08`, simulated) are reserved at the station before the clock starts, so a full pool refuses with 503 instead of leaving a half-run demo. Dispatch never gives a demo driver to a non-demo order, and the burst simulator cannot see them. If a simulator driver already holds the order and has not set off, the demo takes over; a real driver's order is refused.
- **Guards:** `DEV_TOOLS=1` and the fake payment provider, else 404 (a real provider can never enable it); the order's own token, else 403; rate limits per IP and per order (Cloudflare `RATE_LIMIT` binding, in-memory fallback), else 429; a **global daily cap** (`DEMO_DAILY_STEP_CAP`, default 400, one counter row per UTC day, one extra statement per step) else 429; at most 300 steps per order; only non-simulated, paid orders.

**Neon arithmetic.** Measured in PGlite: a typical 6 km run takes 39 steps and about 8.1 store calls (one SQL statement each, roughly) per step; 23 steps and 8.7 per step for 1.5 km; 58 and 7.7 for 11.5 km. A run keeps Neon awake for its length plus the idle tail: 97.5 s + 300 s = 6.6 minutes if runs are far enough apart not to share a tail (the worst case). At the cap, 400 steps / 39 = 10.3 typical runs a day, so at most 10.3 x 6.6 = 68 minutes of compute a day, 34 hours in a 30-day month. The hourly cron already wakes the database 24 times a day (about 5 minutes each, 2 hours a day, 60 hours a month). Worst case total: 94 compute-wall-hours, inside 100 even if every hour awake counted as a full compute-hour. If the compute really is the smallest size (0.25 CU, unverified) that is about 24 CU-hours. Runs that overlap share their tails, so the real number is lower. Not counted: visitors who only browse or place orders, ops console polling, and the page's own 3 s order poll (stopped while a demo runs, and paused by the browser in background tabs). Check Neon's usage page after a week; raise `DEMO_DAILY_STEP_CAP` once the console confirms the size, using hours/month = 30 x cap / 39 x 6.6 / 60.

**Consequences.** The demo moves only while someone watches, at a cost bounded by one number. The driver starts at the station (no approach leg). Event timestamps in the history are real time even though the trip is compressed. The step endpoint is a public, token-protected endpoint that writes to the database; the daily cap is its backstop. Cancelled or abandoned demo orders leave their demo driver "available" at that station until the next run reclaims it; it is never dispatched to other orders. `order_demos` rows (a few KB of route each) are not yet covered by retention (docs/NEXT.md).

**Addendum (quality pass): a per-client budget and a lower per-order ceiling.** The global daily cap was the only thing between one visitor and everyone else's demo: anyone can place orders and pay with the fake provider, so one person with two orders could spend the 400 steps in about 7 minutes at the per-IP rate limit, and every other visitor then got 429 until 00:00 UTC (reproduced as a test before the fix).
- **Per-client daily budget: `DEMO_IP_DAILY_STEPS`, default 120** (a wrangler var). A full run takes about (330 + route seconds) / 25 steps: 39 steps for 6 km, 58 for 11.5 km (measured above), so 120 is two long runs or three typical ones a day per address. At the 400 global cap that is at least four clients to exhaust the day, not one; the global cap stays as the backstop on Neon compute. Finished orders cost nothing.
- **Storage: `demo_usage`, no migration.** Its `day` column is free text, so the counter is the same guarded one-statement upsert as the global one, under the key `<UTC day>|ip|<16 hex of HMAC(ORDER_TOKEN_SECRET, address)>`. One extra statement per step while a demo runs (Neon is awake then anyway). The Workers Rate Limiting binding was not used because it only offers 10 or 60 second windows, and in-memory counters are per isolate, so neither can hold a daily number. The address is hashed so the database holds no visitor IPs. Keys older than two days are deleted by the retention run (`runRetention` now also returns `demoUsage`).
- **Per-order ceiling: `DEMO_MAX_STEPS_PER_ORDER` 300 to 150.** Steps for a route of T seconds are about (330 + T) / 25: 85 for a 30-minute route, 133 for a 60-minute one (about 30 km, well beyond the 12 km radius). 150 covers that, and even two tabs on one order (twice the steps per second, same demo clock) finish a 58-step run in 116. Beyond it the answer is 429 `step_limit` with "This demo has run for as long as it is allowed to."
- A client over its budget gets 429 `ip_daily_cap` with a message in the Run demo panel ("This device has used its demo steps for today (about two full demo runs)...").
- Limits of the design: clients behind one shared address (an office, mobile carrier NAT) share a budget; an attacker with many addresses can still spend the global cap, at a cost of one order and some requests per address.
